Known priorities (taken only from the query)
Small number of meaningful decisions; strategic risk management (not pattern memorization); persistent consequences (injury, death, lost opportunities, rewards); fair surprises; meaningful party/character differences; minimal text + strong visual communication; outcomes that can alter later situations; player has enough information for informed decisions without knowing exact outcomes.
Research sources and findings (selected primary or high-quality secondary)
- Suspense / systems suspense: Lehne & Koelsch (tension/suspense model); Alexei Pepers & Caves of Qud discussions (systems suspense = active awareness of fuzzy edges in the player’s mental model of the rules); CGMagazine and academic reviews of suspense management (anticipation + uncertainty + delayed resolution). Evidence level: mixture of psychological models and designer reports.
- Push-your-luck / greed / regret: Can’t Stop, Incan Gold/Diamant, Quacks of Quedlinburg, Deep Sea Adventure analyses (Board Game Design Course, Space Slug Games, Goomba Stomp). Causal link: repeated “bank or risk” decision + visible accumulation + non-calculable or emotionally weighted odds + sunk-cost pressure produces temptation, anticipation, and post-decision regret. Near-miss activates reward circuitry (Clark et al. line of research). Failure modes well documented: when the choice is forced by other players or expected value is always transparent, tension collapses.
- Risk management under incomplete information: competitive FPS/economy systems (VALORANT, CS2 analyses) and board-game uncertainty frameworks (Kyle Ip chapter). Players constantly weigh “what can I afford to lose.”
- Consequential sacrifice / fear for characters: Lisa: The Painful (permanent limb loss that changes gameplay), Scarlet Hollow (costly altruism that ages/weakens the protagonist), Soul Sacrifice (explicit sacrifice-for-power vs release), Pathologic (resource scarcity forces triage). Evidence is primarily designer intent + player reception, not controlled studies.
- Meaningful / interesting decisions: Sid Meier’s oft-cited framing (series of interesting decisions), Roadwarden postmortem (impact and variance through reputation, burnt bridges, non-reversible outcomes), Pattern Language for Game Design (mystery-driven exploration, incomplete information as motivation).
- Fair surprise: systems that set up a mental model and then sharply revise it (Pepers definition of surprise vs suspense).
Distinctions
- Evidence: psychological mechanisms of near-miss, loss aversion, and anticipation; documented player reports and postmortems of specific games.
- Plausible design hypothesis: most mappings of feeling → sequence below.
- Inference: applicability to a compact, turn-based, party-management RPG with persistent injury/death.
Conflicting evidence is preserved: high uncertainty can produce frustration as easily as suspense; near-miss can motivate continued play while reducing long-term satisfaction; “interesting decisions” is not a universal requirement (works better for strategy than pure puzzle or pure narrative).
2. Feeling → Gameplay Pattern Map
Each entry follows the required structure.
Suspense / Anticipation
Sequence: Player commits to an action whose full consequences are delayed or revealed in stages; intermediate signals (visual, state change) keep attention on the uncertain edge of the mental model.
Information: Partial (player knows the range of possible outcomes and some probabilities or patterns, not the exact result).
Why meaningful: The commitment is irreversible or costly to reverse; the player has levers that change the probability distribution.
Success / partial / failure: Success resolves with relief and confirmation of the model; partial keeps the uncertainty alive; failure revises the model (fair surprise).
Example: Staggered revelation or systems with “regular vs rare” outcomes (Caves of Qud style); delayed resolution in competitive economy rounds.
Support: Psychological models of anticipation + designer analyses of systems suspense.
Failure modes: Outcome arrives too fast (no anticipation), or uncertainty is pure noise with no learnable edge (feels random, not suspenseful).
Greed / Temptation / Regret
Sequence: Accumulate temporary gains; after each increment face an explicit “bank what I have or risk it for more” decision.
Information: Visible pile of gains + visible or inferred rising risk.
Why meaningful: Sunk-cost pressure + non-trivial chance of total or partial loss; player owns the decision.
Success: Bank early (safe but lower reward) or successfully push (high reward + pride). Failure: Bust and watch the pile vanish (regret). Partial: Keep some, lose some.
Example: Can’t Stop (roll or stop), Incan Gold (leave the temple or stay).
Support: Strong designer consensus + near-miss neuroscience.
Failure modes: Expected value always transparent → pure calculation, no emotion; external forces remove the choice → no ownership of regret.
Fear for a character / Willingness to sacrifice
Sequence: A character is exposed to escalating or targeted risk; player can reallocate protection, consumables, or accept permanent cost to save them.
Information: Clear differential exposure (slot, role, or status) and visible cost of intervention.
Why meaningful: Attachment is built by prior investment; the sacrifice changes future capabilities or party composition.
Success: Character survives at a cost. Failure: Permanent injury/death that alters later options. Partial: Character lives but is degraded.
Example: Lisa: The Painful (arm loss), Scarlet Hollow (costly altruism), Soul Sacrifice.
Support: Player reception and designer interviews (mostly qualitative).
Failure modes: Characters are interchangeable or death is instantly reversed → no fear; cost is purely numerical with no gameplay change → cosmetic.
Relief after danger
Sequence: Sustained pressure (timer, rising alarm, accumulating risk) ends with a clear resolution point the player can influence.
Information: Player sees the pressure rising and has at least one lever to affect the exit conditions.
Why meaningful: The relief is proportional to the preceding uncertainty and personal investment.
Example: Successfully banking in a push-your-luck run or extracting after a high-alarm phase.
Failure modes: Pressure is purely cosmetic or the exit is automatic.
Fair surprise
Sequence: Player builds a mental model of how the system behaves; an outcome sharply revises that model in a way that is retrospectively coherent.
Information: Enough history and feedback for the player to have formed expectations.
Why meaningful: The revision opens or closes real options.
Example: Systems suspense turning into surprise (Pepers); rare but signalled outcomes.
Failure modes: Pure randomness or “gotcha” that could not have been anticipated even in principle.
Cleverness / Mastery / Satisfaction
Sequence: Player reads partial information or patterns, forms a hypothesis, acts on it, and sees the hypothesis confirmed or productively refined.
Information: Observable history + incomplete but structured data.
Why meaningful: Outcome depends on the quality of the read more than pure luck.
Example: Reading an opponent’s pattern in a repeated contested choice; using recon to change approach.
Failure modes: Patterns are either noise or completely deterministic.
Curiosity / Desire to discover
Sequence: Incomplete but compelling information is presented; player has concrete actions that reveal more.
Information: Partial knowledge that raises specific questions.
Why meaningful: Revelation changes strategy or stakes, not just flavour.
Example: Mystery-driven exploration pattern (Pattern Language for Game Design).
Failure modes: Revelation is only text or does not affect decisions.
Desperation / Escalating pressure / Difficult trade-offs
Sequence: Multiple resources or goals are under simultaneous pressure; protecting one worsens another.
Information: Clear visibility of the trade-off.
Why meaningful: No dominant safe option; consequences persist.
Example: Pathologic-style scarcity; multi-resource push-your-luck.
Failure modes: One resource is obviously more important → no real trade-off.
3. Comparative Shortlist (3–5 most promising for RpgBalancer)
Evaluated only against the stated priorities and the absence of verified implementation details.
Bank-or-Risk Accumulation (classic push-your-luck)
Emotional potential: High for greed, temptation, regret, anticipation, relief.
Decision quality: Single repeated high-stakes binary (or low-N) choice; strong agency.
Replayability: High via different risk tolerances and near-miss variance.
Compatibility: Can sit on top of existing progress/HP/loot checks with modest new state (temporary pile + bust condition).
Cost: Low–medium.
Risks: If odds become fully transparent or the choice is forced, emotion collapses. Monte Carlo can validate mechanical distributions; human playtest needed for felt regret/temptation.
Targeted Exposure + Sacrifice Choice
Emotional potential: High for fear-for-character, willingness to sacrifice, attachment, relief.
Decision quality: Clear differential risk by slot/character + costly intervention.
Replayability: Strong via party composition differences.
Compatibility: Directly leverages existing slot/risk/HP/condition systems.
Cost: Low if slot differentiation already exists.
Risks: If characters feel interchangeable, fear disappears. Simulation can check injury distributions; playtest for attachment.
Incomplete-Information Commitment (recon → irreversible approach)
Emotional potential: Curiosity, cleverness, fair surprise, suspense.
Decision quality: Gather limited info, then commit to a strategy whose full consequences unfold later.
Replayability: High if info reveals different viable approaches.
Compatibility: Builds on existing perception/intelligence checks and hidden-info ideas.
Cost: Medium (needs non-numeric revelation that changes options).
Risks: Info feels like a pure bonus rather than a strategic pivot. Human playtest essential for “fairness in retrospect.”
Escalating Multi-Resource Pressure
Emotional potential: Desperation, difficult trade-offs, relief.
Decision quality: Simultaneous protection of progress, party integrity, and exit option.
Compatibility: Requires clear visual state for multiple meters; may need new escalation primitives.
Cost: Medium–high.
Risks: Dominant strategy or analysis paralysis. Monte Carlo useful for balance of pressures; playtest for felt desperation.
Repeated Contested Choice with Persistent Score (RPS-like or similar)
Emotional potential: Suspense, anticipation, cleverness, rimonta, decisive moment.
Decision quality: Series of active choices with readable score.
Compatibility: Requires new opponent-behaviour and score state; less direct fit to pure risk-management priority.
Cost: Medium.
Risks: Collapses to random or pure memory if opponent is not designed carefully. Lower priority under the stated “strategic risk management rather than memorizing patterns” constraint.
4. Goblin Quest Alternatives
Treated only after the broader research, as one candidate among others. No verified implementation details exist, so analysis is limited to the structure described in prior conversation (linear phases, checks, possible push-your-luck loop).
Plausible current feelings: basic risk, some near-miss excitement if repeated checks exist, consequences via injury/loot.
Unsupported or weak: deep greed/regret loops, strong character-specific fear/sacrifice, systems suspense from fuzzy mental models, cleverness from reading incomplete but structured information.
Alternative A – Bank-or-Risk Temple (does not rely on combat as core)
Player assigns party to an exploration run. Temporary loot/progress pile grows with each successful step. After every step the player faces an explicit “bank and leave” vs “continue deeper” decision. Continuing raises a visible danger meter; a bust condition (second matching hazard, alarm threshold, or failed group check) loses the temporary pile and may injure exposed characters. Party composition and consumables affect how far one can safely push and who is at risk. Outcome alters later situations (different resources available, injured characters, or new threats from high alarm).
Feelings targeted: greed, temptation, regret, anticipation, relief, some character fear.
Alternative B – Targeted Sacrifice Extraction (materially different structure)
Goblin site is under rising pressure. Player must extract a valuable objective while deciding, under incomplete information, which characters to expose to high-risk roles (rear-guard, scout, carrier). At key moments the player can choose to sacrifice position, a consumable, or accept permanent injury on one character to protect another or the objective. No repeated “push” loop; instead a small number of irreversible allocation and sacrifice decisions whose consequences cascade. Information is partial (perception reveals some but not all threat vectors).
Feelings targeted: fear for characters, willingness to sacrifice, difficult trade-offs, relief, fair surprise if the revealed threat revises the plan.
5. Recommended Next Experiment
Smallest discriminating prototype:
A single-session, paper or spreadsheet prototype of Alternative A (Bank-or-Risk) versus a pure repeated-check baseline of similar length and stakes.
Protocol:
- 6–10 players, each runs both versions (counter-balanced).
- Record: decision points, whether the player changed strategy after information or near-miss, final outcome, and immediate post-run ratings (temptation felt, regret, suspense, perceived agency, desire to try again).
- Open question: “What were you thinking the moment you decided to stop / continue / expose a character?”
This discriminates whether the explicit bank-or-risk decision sequence produces the targeted feelings more reliably than the existing check-driven structure, without requiring new production code or full quest implementation. Human playtest is necessary; Monte Carlo can only validate the mechanical distributions of outcomes, not the emotional response.
